What British means to us

Four things a good British room has

Not a style to copy, but a way of looking at a house. These are what we check for before we draw anything.

Concept image
Proportion

Every part answers to another

Georgian builders set rooms out by rule, and later builders kept many of the habits. Panels, doors, windows and cornices answer to each other. New joinery should share those proportions, not fight them.

Concept image
Useful rooms

Practical first

Boot rooms, pantries, sculleries and libraries exist because country life needs them. The joinery is there to make the room work, and the beauty follows from doing that well.

Concept image
Enduring materials

Chosen for how they age

Oak that darkens with light, paint that can be touched up, brass that wears to the hand. We choose materials for how they will look in twenty years, not on the day we hand over.

Concept image
Layers of character

Several periods at once

A Georgian front, a Victorian wing, a 1970s kitchen. We keep what is good, repair where we can, and add the next layer honestly.

Proportion

Read the house before changing it

Many Georgian rooms were set out from simple geometry: a square, its diagonal, and the rectangle that grows from it. Windows, panels and doors follow the same order. New joinery that ignores it tends to look added on.

  1. Start from the module: the width of the room, the window or the panel.
  2. Swing the diagonal of the square to find the height.
  3. Divide by the stiles, rails and glazing bars.
  4. Fit the new piece to the same order, then to the way you live.
A guide by period

Panelling and detail, from Georgian to Edwardian

Dates, the houses you'll find each style in, the profiles and finishes, and how we interpret them today. A house's date is a starting point; what survives in it, and what was changed later, matters more.

c. 1714 – 1830

Georgian

Order and restraint. Rooms are organised like a classical column: the dado as pedestal, the wall as shaft, the cornice as entablature.

Where you'll find itTown houses, rectories, farmhouses refronted in brick or stone, country houses.
Profiles
Early: full-height raised-and-fielded panelling. Later: dado-height panelling with ovolo and astragal details.
Finish
Painted softwood; oak in older or grander rooms.
Colour
StoneDrabPea greenOff-white
Evidence
Surviving panelling, paint scrapes, and pattern books such as Batty Langley's.
Our take
Wardrobes set within the dado line, with ovolo-moulded in-frame doors.
c. 1800 – 1837

Regency

Lighter, more linear and more Greek. Panelling gives way to plaster walls with a dado rail; detail moves to doors and architraves.

Where you'll find itStucco terraces and villas; Bath, Cheltenham and Brighton.
Profiles
Reeded architraves with corner blocks, slender sash bars, Greek key motifs.
Finish
Painted softwood; mahogany with brass stringing in furniture.
Colour
Pale greyLilac pinkBuffDeep green
Evidence
Surviving terraces and villas, and published designs.
Our take
Alcove cabinets with a reeded pilaster and corner block.
1837 – 1901

Victorian

Richer, heavier and more various. Mass production made deep mouldings affordable, and every room was expected to look its part.

Where you'll find itTerraces, villas, rectories, estate cottages and extended farmhouses.
Profiles
Deep skirtings, ogee and torus profiles, four-panel doors, picture rails.
Finish
Softwood grained to look like oak; varnished pitch pine.
Colour
Deep redBottle greenBrownOchre
Evidence
Builders' catalogues and graining found under later paint.
Our take
A stained-oak media unit whose plinth picks up the skirting height.
c. 1860 – 1914

Arts & Crafts

Honest construction, shown rather than hidden, and the style most at home in the countryside.

Where you'll find itCountry houses, Cotswold villages, rural cottages and inglenook halls.
Profiles
Boarded or simply framed panelling, chamfers with stops, exposed tenons and pegs.
Finish
Solid oak, waxed, fumed or limed; hand-forged iron hardware.
Colour
Waxed oakSageOchreLimewash
Evidence
Surviving houses and the movement's published drawings.
Our take
A solid oak boot room with the pegged joints left visible.
1901 – c. 1914

Edwardian

Lighter and airier, with white woodwork and more daylight. Arts & Crafts and Queen Anne influences side by side.

Where you'll find itSuburban villas, country houses and arts-influenced cottages.
Profiles
Simpler torus skirtings, high picture rails and plate shelves, part-glazed doors.
Finish
White or cream enamel paint on softwood; oak in halls.
Colour
CreamSoft greenWhiteMushroom
Evidence
Surviving houses, trade catalogues and paint scrapes.
Our take
Painted wardrobes with a cornice at plate-shelf height.
