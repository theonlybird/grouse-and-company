What British means to us

Four things a good British room has

Not a style to copy, but a way of looking at a house. These are what we check for before we draw anything.

Proportion

Georgian builders set rooms out by rule, and later builders kept many of the habits. Panels, doors, windows and cornices answer to each other. New joinery should share those proportions, not fight them.

Concept

Useful rooms

Boot rooms, pantries, sculleries and libraries exist because country life needs them. A British interior is practical first, and the joinery is there to make the room work.

Concept

Enduring materials

Oak that darkens with light, paint that can be touched up, brass that wears to the hand. We choose materials for how they will look in twenty years, not on the day we hand over.

Concept

Layers of character

Most old houses are several periods at once: a Georgian front, a Victorian wing, a 1970s kitchen. We keep what is good, repair where we can, and add the next layer honestly.

Proportion

Read the house before changing it

Many Georgian rooms were set out from simple geometry: a square, its diagonal, and the rectangle that grows from it. Windows, panels and doors follow the same order. New joinery that ignores it tends to look added on.

  1. Start from the module: the width of the room, the window or the panel.
  2. Swing the diagonal of the square to find the height.
  3. Divide by the stiles, rails and glazing bars.
  4. Fit the new piece to the same order, then to the way you live.

c. 1714 – 1830

Georgian

Order and restraint. Rooms are organised like a classical column: the dado as pedestal, the wall as shaft, the cornice as entablature.

Where you'll find itTown houses, rectories, farmhouses refronted in brick or stone, country houses.
Profiles
Early: full-height raised-and-fielded panelling with bolection moulds. Later: panelling drops to dado height, with ovolo and astragal details and a plaster wall above.
Finish
Painted softwood (deal), with oak in older or grander rooms.
Colour
StoneDrabPea greenOff-white
Evidence
Surviving panelling, paint scrapes, and builders' pattern books such as Batty Langley's and William Pain's.
Our take
Wardrobes set within the dado line, ovolo-moulded in-frame doors, and a cornice that returns into the room's own.

c. 1800 – 1837

Regency

Lighter, more linear and more Greek. Panelling largely gives way to plaster walls with a dado rail; detail moves to doors, architraves and shutters.

Where you'll find itStucco terraces and villas, and the spa and seaside towns: Bath, Cheltenham, Brighton.
Profiles
Reeded architraves with corner blocks, slender sash bars, Greek key and anthemion motifs. Shallow, crisp mouldings.
Finish
Painted softwood; mahogany and rosewood in furniture, with brass stringing.
Colour
Pale greyLilac pinkBuffDeep green
Evidence
Surviving terraces and villas, and the published designs of the period.
Our take
Alcove cabinets with a reeded pilaster and a square corner block, painted to match the walls.
Concept image
1837 – 1901

Victorian

Richer, heavier and more various. Mass production made deep mouldings affordable, and every room was expected to look its part.

Where you'll find itTerraces, villas, rectories, estate cottages and extended farmhouses.
Profiles
Deep skirtings in principal rooms, ogee and torus profiles, four-panel doors, picture rails, and dados finished with Lincrusta or tiles in halls.
Finish
Softwood grained to look like oak or mahogany; varnished pitch pine; dark stained timber.
Colour
Deep redBottle greenBrownOchre
Evidence
Builders' catalogues, surviving graining under later paint, and period photographs.
Our take
A media unit in stained oak with a deep plinth that picks up the skirting height.
Concept image
c. 1860 – 1914

Arts & Crafts

Honest construction, shown rather than hidden. A reaction against machine-made ornament, and the style most at home in the countryside.

Where you'll find itCountry houses, Cotswold villages, rural cottages and inglenook halls.
Profiles
Plain boarded or simply framed panelling, chamfers with stops, exposed through-tenons and pegs, built-in settles and inglenooks.
Finish
Solid oak, waxed, fumed or limed; hand-forged iron and copper hardware.
Colour
Waxed oakSageOchreLimewash
Evidence
Surviving houses by architects of the movement, and their published drawings.
Our take
A boot room in solid oak with pegged joints left visible and a bench made to be sat on.
Concept image
1901 – c. 1914

Edwardian

Lighter and airier after the Victorians, with white woodwork and more daylight. Arts & Crafts and Queen Anne influences sit side by side.

Where you'll find itSuburban villas, country houses and arts-influenced cottages.
Profiles
Simpler torus skirtings, high picture rails and plate shelves, square-section balusters, and part-glazed doors.
Finish
White or cream enamel paint on softwood; oak in halls and dining rooms.
Colour
CreamSoft greenWhiteMushroom
Evidence
Surviving houses, trade catalogues and paint scrapes.
Our take
Painted wardrobes with a plate-shelf height cornice and a single bead on the door frame.
